# Transgender psychology — practical notes for the love guru

Use these notes when either person is trans, non-binary or questioning. Never guess; follow the words they use for themselves.

## Core needs
- **Being seen as who you are.** Correct name and pronouns are not a favour, they are the baseline. A partner who gets it right in private but misgenders in public has created a safety problem, not a slip.
- **Safety.** Physical safety, and the safety of not being outed to family, colleagues or landlords. Never pressure someone to disclose.
- **Dignity in intimacy.** Body dysphoria can make touch, mirrors, sex and photographs complicated. Ask what is welcome rather than assuming, and never treat a boundary as a rejection.
- **Autonomy over transition.** Medical, legal and social steps have their own timeline. A partner's job is support, not steering.

## Typical communication style
- Many trans people have had to explain themselves constantly; they may be tired of educating. Do your own reading, then ask about their specific experience.
- They often read micro-signals accurately, because they have had to. Being told "you're overthinking it" is dismissive.
- Directness about needs is common and should be met with directness, not delicacy.
- Grief and euphoria can both appear around transition. Both deserve room.

## Common friction patterns
- **Misgendering as a pattern.** A repeated "accident" that never improves reads as low effort. What matters is the response: correction, no defensiveness, no lecture about how hard it is.
- **Partner's discomfort with visibility.** One partner wants to be open about the relationship; the other fears family or workplace consequences. This needs a concrete plan, not shame.
- **Deadnaming by relatives**, and the partner who stays silent while it happens. Silence feels like complicity.
- **Chaser dynamics and fetishisation.** Being desired for a body part rather than as a person is a specific and common wound in dating.
- **Medication, surgery and energy.** Hormones, recovery and dysphoria can affect mood, libido and stamina. Fatigue is not disinterest.
- **Disclosure anxiety.** Fear of rejection makes some people delay honesty, which then looks like deception when it surfaces.

## What builds attraction and closeness
- Consistency: correct language, correction without defensiveness, and doing it in front of other people.
- Desire expressed in ways that are not centred on the body parts they feel unsure about; ask what feels good.
- Advocating for them when they are not in the room.
- Curiosity about their life and interests beyond transition.
- Patience with hard days, without making the relationship a caretaking project.
- Explicit commitment: saying clearly that you are in this, not just "seeing how it goes".

## What kills it
- "You're being too sensitive." It ends trust quickly.
- Treating transition as a negotiation or a threat to the relationship.
- Asking invasive questions about surgery, genitals or medical history.
- Using their identity as ammunition in an argument.
- Publicly describing them with the wrong words to keep family comfortable.
- Making them responsible for educating you.

## How to open them up
1. State plainly that you are not going anywhere and this is not about their identity being a problem.
2. Use their words for themselves, exactly as they use them.
3. Ask a specific question about what they need from you this week.
4. Take responsibility for your own learning without making it their homework.
5. Follow through visibly; consistency is the whole message.

## When writing drafts
Warm, plain, respectful, and never clinical. Use the names and pronouns they use. Acknowledge the feeling, own your part, and offer something concrete. Avoid gendered assumptions in compliments, avoid "biological" framing, and never debate identity.
