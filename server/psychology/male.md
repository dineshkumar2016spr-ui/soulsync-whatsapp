# Male psychology — reference notes for the love guru

Synthesized from: Wilson et al. (2025) "Suicidality in Men Following Relationship Breakdown"; Aparicio-García et al. (2026) CMNI-29 masculine norms across the lifespan; Seikowski, Stöbe & Harth (2008) Leipzig aging study; Whitley & Barry on midlife male distress; Terrence Real (1998) covert male depression; Miller (2012) personality mediators of attachment; Pomianek (2026) ARC cybernetic attachment model; Camacho-Ruiz et al. (2026) narrative review of men's mental health and stigma; Oliffe et al. (2024) photovoice "Men building intimate partner relationships"; Burns et al. (2026) gender norm conformity and help-seeking. Use these notes whenever the other person is a man.

## Core needs
- **Respect and status before affection.** A man reads correction, sarcasm or comparison in front of others as an attack on status. He will go quiet or defensive rather than admit hurt.
- **Competence and usefulness.** He wants to feel useful, not managed. "I need your help with X" lands far better than "you never do X".
- **Space to process.** Most men need hours, sometimes a day, before they can name a feeling. Pushing for an answer in the moment produces shutdown.
- **Being chosen openly.** He wants evidence he is wanted: initiation, physical touch, specific appreciation, being introduced as a partner.
- **A sense of agency.** Shared decision-making and control over his own recovery keep him engaged. Removing agency produces resistance.

## Developmental context (Levinson, Arnett, CMNI-29)
- Emerging adulthood (18-29): identity exploration, instability, self-focus, feeling in-between, age of possibilities. Younger men conform more strongly to masculine norms.
- CMNI-29 norms decline with age: risk-taking, self-reliance, winning, violence and heterosexual self-presentation drop significantly from emerging to older adulthood (β = -0.39 to -0.18). Emotional control does NOT decline (β = +0.02). Precarious Manhood Theory: younger men continually demonstrate fragile masculine status through competitiveness and risk-taking; stability and identity consolidation later reduce this.
- Midlife transition (40-45): terminating early adulthood, initiating middle adulthood, resolving polarities (destructive/constructive, attached/autonomous). The "midlife crisis" is emotional turbulence here specifically.
- Age-50 dip: life satisfaction drops sharply at 48-55 (leisure, partnership, sexuality, self-satisfaction, social integration) then recovers by 56-65 with psychological reorientation. Common complaints: concentration disorders (72.9%), fatigue (70.6%), irritability (65.9%), failing memory (63.5%), joint/limb pain (77.6%), back pain (68.2%), diminished sexual interest (62.6%).
- Men derive a large part of identity, self-worth and social meaning from work and the provider role. Job loss, debt and foreclosure strip this and precipitate existential despair.

## Typical communication style
- Short messages are often not coldness; they are how he texts by default. Judge him on consistency and action, not message length.
- He solves problems when you want comfort. If she says "I'm exhausted", he hears "fix it". Ask directly: "I don't need a solution right now, I need you to listen."
- He withdraws under criticism. Complaint phrased as a character flaw ("you're selfish") triggers defence; complaint phrased as an unmet need ("I felt alone last night") gets a response.
- Many men only say the emotional thing once, quietly, then drop it. It was probably important.
- Two people who both default to independence can drift into a flat, roommate dynamic without anyone raising it.

## Masculine norms and mental health (stigma, help-seeking)
- Traditional masculine norms (emotional repression, self-reliance, "being strong") conceal distress, delay treatment, and help explain higher burdens of addiction, violence and suicide alongside lower recorded affective/anxiety diagnoses.
- Hegemonic masculinity is acquired through gender-differentiated socialization; it is not biological. Men's psychological distress is often expressed externally (irritability, risk-taking, substance misuse, aggression) rather than internally (sadness, crying, guilt).
- Attitudinal barriers: self-stigma, shame, symptom minimization, mistrust. Structural barriers: limited tailored services, navigation difficulties, costs, bureaucracy.
- "Last resort" model: many men endure suffering alone, deny it, and only seek help when in crisis, often via their closest environment.
- Positive masculinity: self-reliance can be reframed as resilience and emotional coping; treatment should incorporate shared decision-making and agency.
- Caring masculinity (values positive emotion and relationality over domination) and hybrid masculinity (expression shaped by sociocultural context) are underutilised but promising.
- Feminine norm conformity in men (modesty, kindness, nurturing, care provision, prioritising relationships) is linked to increased help-seeking and treatment engagement. Women have more positive help-seeking attitudes.

## Covert male depression (Terrence Real)
- Societal conditioning instructing boys to suppress vulnerability forces male depression underground, where it manifests as irritability, anger, workaholism, substance abuse and emotional withdrawal.
- Unaddressed covert depression severely damages marital intimacy and accelerates relationship breakdown during midlife transitions.

## Adult attachment (Miller, Pomianek ARC)
- Anxious attachment: deep need for close relationships, profound fear of abandonment, loss of romantic connections felt as particularly aversive.
- Avoidant attachment: uncomfortable with emotional closeness, prioritises independence.
- Relationship satisfaction predictors (R2 = .37): ECR Anxiety (β = -0.393), ECR Avoidance (β = -0.372), NEO Agreeableness (β = +0.159), AAS Dependence (β = +0.128), AAS Anxiety (β = +0.059), NEO Neuroticism (β = -0.033).
- Men report higher CPI Independence, higher comfort with closeness (AAS Close), and higher NEO Openness than women. Women show more anxious relational style.
- ARC (cybernetic) model: momentary relational discrepancy e = desired closeness - perceived closeness; feedback loop through emotion, action, partner response, recovery/persistence. Attachment dynamics are micro-temporal and self-regulating.
- Early attachment styles remain moderately stable across the lifespan and shape partner selection, reactions to threats and vulnerability to suicide.

## Relationship breakdown and suicidality (Wilson et al.)
- Separated/divorced men have greater odds of suicidal ideation (OR = 1.64), suicide attempt (OR = 1.73) and death by suicide (OR = 2.82) than married men.
- Risk is greatest in the immediate aftermath: separated men have nearly twofold greater odds of suicide than divorced men (OR = 1.96); separated men under 35 have over 8x the odds.
- Risk factors: younger age (≤34), less than tertiary education, lack of employment, loneliness, emotion dysregulation, isolation, shame.
- Divorce is precipitated by chronic strain (interpersonal, financial, housing) and acute emotional upheaval. Adaptively managed, post-crisis growth is possible.
- Cross-cultural: higher suicide risk among divorced men in Asian relative to non-Asian countries (shame, fathers' fluctuating contact with children).

## Midlife male distress (Whitley, Barry)
- Middle-aged men (40-60) have the highest suicide rate of any demographic in Western society.
- Family breakdown and restricted contact with children are the single most potent acute drivers: unmarried/divorced middle-aged men are 3.5x more likely to die by suicide; over 80% of custodial parents are mothers; loss of contact (often via parental alienation) leaves pain open and unhealed.
- Men rely on their wife for primary emotional intimacy; post-divorce they experience acute social isolation while women maintain peripheral ties.
- Macro-economic shift (industrial to knowledge economy) devastated blue-collar male employment; suicide rates among middle-aged men with high school or less are 2.4x higher than college-educated.
- Male-friendly interventions: action-oriented problem solving, shoulder-to-shoulder activities (Men's Sheds, sports, walking in nature), existential therapy focused on fatherhood resilience and meaning. Avoid pathologising male behaviour as "toxic masculinity".

## Men building intimate partner relationships (Oliffe et al. photovoice, 19-43)
- Balancing autonomy and coupledom: men seek to retain individual agency while meeting joint expectations; autonomy cast as mutual agreement is a primary connector. Can be challenging as partnerships progress (leisure, domesticities, friendships).
- Building compatibility and mutuality: congruence with partner as foundation; mutuality as jointly bringing about gender equality and equity to sustain through ups and downs.
- Communicating intimacy: regular discussions to express affection and understand/meet each other's sexual and emotional needs; closeness garnered by intentional secluded couple-oriented experiences.
- Marriage/cohabitation protect men's mental health; break-ups raise distress, depression, lower self-esteem, suicidality and substance use. Men who have rebuilt 2+ partnerships show the best mental health.
- Best upstream support: strength-based relationship coaching, not only corrective/mandated services.

## Common friction patterns (men)
- Pursue-withdraw: she raises an issue, he feels attacked, goes silent, she escalates, he withdraws further. Break it by naming the loop, not the person.
- Scorekeeping over effort: he feels practical contributions (work, fixing, driving) go unseen; she feels emotional contributions missing. Both real, different currencies.
- Delayed honesty: he avoids conflict by agreeing then not following through. The mismatch between words and action is the actual wound.
- Phone as retreat: scrolling is usually self-soothing after a stressful day, not a statement about her.
- Unexpressed insecurity about relationship status: one assumes exclusivity, the other assumes nothing agreed. Say the terms out loud.
- Outness/visibility mismatch, HIV/sexual health silence, family and chosen-family rejection, internalised shame projected onto partner.

## What builds attraction and closeness (men)
- Confidence without pressure: certainty about what he wants, calm when she is upset.
- Warm, specific appreciation: "I loved that you handled the booking" beats generic praise.
- Playfulness and teasing that never lands on an insecurity.
- Reliability and follow-through (single strongest signal).
- A life of his own: neediness reads as low value; independence plus clear interest is magnetic.
- Physical affection outside of sex; being wanted openly (public acknowledgement, meeting friends, introduced as partner).
- Emotional bravery: being first to say "I like you, I'm not seeing anyone else".
- Pride in being together: photos, introductions, plans made in advance.
- Shared life outside the bedroom: fitness, food, friends, travel.
- Sexual attentiveness and communication without performance pressure.

## What kills it (men)
- Nagging, contempt, eye-rolling, bringing up past failures in a fight.
- Comparing him to another man (ex, friend's partner, stranger).
- Silent treatment (reads as punishment, produces distance).
- Making him beg for clarity about where the relationship stands.
- Treating him as a convenience: late-night texts only, plans cancelled last minute.
- Using his outness, family or body as ammunition.
- Stonewalling and disappearing for days.
- Public flirtation that humiliates him.

## How to open a man up
1. Low-stakes timing: side by side (walk, drive), not face to face across a table.
2. Lead with one honest sentence about your own feeling, not his behaviour.
3. Ask one specific question: "What was the hardest part of this week?" not "how do you feel?"
4. Reflect back what he said before adding your piece.
5. Give him a next step he can act on — action is how he shows love.
6. Say explicitly that the conversation is safe and not a breakup conversation.
7. Lead with your own vulnerability first; he will match the level you set.
8. Confirm relationship terms concretely: what you both want, what you are not doing with others.

## Safety and risk
- Men are at elevated suicide risk after relationship breakdown, especially separated men under 35, with low education/employment, loneliness and emotion dysregulation. Shame and isolation are central.
- If a user describes suicidal thoughts, hopelessness, self-harm, or a partner who is coercive/abusive, the guru must respond with care, name the risk plainly, and point to professional support and crisis lines (e.g. 988 in the US, local equivalents) rather than a clever text. Never advise manipulation, deception, threats, guilt-tripping, love-bombing, stalking, or anything that removes someone's choice.
- Men's help-seeking is lowest when masculine norms are rigidly held; reframe vulnerability as strength and offer agency and shoulder-to-shoulder support.

## When writing his reply drafts
Keep it short, direct, warm and free of interrogation. One clear statement, one question at most, no paragraph-length confession, no sarcasm, no "we need to talk" opener unless a real conversation is genuinely required. Acknowledge her feeling in one sentence, own your part in one, then offer one concrete next step or a real question. Never argue with a feeling, never use "but" straight after "sorry", avoid long justifications.
